# Codex Build Plan: Break It, Build It
## Science Word Parts for TEKS, NGSS, and SST Readiness

Build a complete, self-contained, privacy-first website and printable handout library for teaching high-utility science prefixes, roots, and suffixes across grades 3–5, 6–8, and 9–12.

The finished resource should feel similar in purpose and usability to:

https://mglearn.github.io/activities/sayitmakeit/

Do not copy that site's code or exact visual design. Recreate the useful interaction pattern:

- Quick browsing
- Clear grade-band categories
- Small, reusable activities
- Teacher-friendly printing
- Minimal setup
- No accounts
- No student data
- Works on phones, Chromebooks, laptops, and classroom printers

Working title:

# Break It, Build It
### Science Word Parts

Tagline:

**Decode the word. Connect the science. Apply what you know.**

---

# 1. Core Purpose

Create a science morphology resource that helps students use Greek and Latin prefixes, roots, and suffixes to make sense of unfamiliar science vocabulary.

The resource must go beyond memorizing definitions.

Students should progressively learn to:

1. Recognize useful word parts
2. Explain what those parts mean
3. Combine parts to predict the meaning of a science term
4. Check that prediction against scientific context
5. Use word-part knowledge while reading diagrams, passages, models, observations, and data
6. Transfer known word-part knowledge to unfamiliar terminology

The site should support:

- Core instruction
- Vocabulary development
- Bellringers
- Stations
- Intervention
- Small-group instruction
- Science notebooks
- Partner activities
- Exit tickets
- SST preparation
- Multilingual learner support
- Independent practice

Do not make this a collection of traditional vocabulary worksheets.

# 4. SST + ACE Learning Progression

Use this local instructional progression throughout the site.

## BOY: Articulate

Students demonstrate prior and surface knowledge.

Typical morphology tasks:

- Recognize a prefix
- Identify a root
- Identify a suffix
- Match a word part with a meaning
- Explain a familiar science term in their own words
- Use context to confirm a simple inference

Prompt style:

**What does this word part tell you?**

Example:

GEO = Earth

What clue does GEO give you about the word GEOTHERMAL?

---

## MOY: Connect

Students combine information and explain relationships.

Typical morphology tasks:

- Combine two or more morphemes
- Connect word structure to science meaning
- Use morphology plus a diagram
- Use morphology plus a passage
- Use morphology plus observations
- Explain why a predicted meaning fits the scientific context

Prompt style:

**How do the word parts and the science evidence work together?**

---

## EOY: Extend

Students transfer word-part knowledge to unfamiliar terminology and situations.

Typical tasks:

- Decode a new science term
- Apply a familiar morpheme in a new context
- Interpret an unfamiliar stimulus
- Make and justify a prediction
- Compare two related terms
- Explain when morphology helps and when context must override a literal translation

Prompt style:

**Use what you know to figure out something you have not seen before.**

---

# 5. ACE Integration

Include ACE selectively throughout activities.

## A: Articulate

Students:

- Name the word parts
- Explain their meanings
- Restate a science term in their own words

Prompt examples:

- What does each word part mean?
- Say the term in your own words.
- Which word part gives you the strongest clue?

## C: Connect

Students:

- Connect morphology to scientific meaning
- Connect the term to a concept, model, process, or observation
- Explain why the word parts fit

Prompt examples:

- How do these word parts connect?
- How does the word structure match the science?
- What evidence confirms your prediction?

## E: Extend

Students:

- Decode unfamiliar terms
- Generate related science vocabulary
- Apply the morpheme to a novel situation
- Explain limitations of literal word-part decoding

Prompt examples:

- Find another science word containing this root.
- Predict what this unfamiliar word might mean.
- Where else might this word part appear in science?

# 6. SOLO Connection

Use SOLO as a teacher-facing interpretation layer.

Do not label students by SOLO level.

Describe the complexity shown in the response.

Suggested four-point shorthand:

## Level 1: One Clue

Student identifies one relevant word part.

Approximate SOLO connection:

- Uni-structural

## Level 2: Several Clues

Student recognizes multiple pieces but treats them separately.

Approximate SOLO connection:

- Multi-structural

## Level 3: Connected Meaning

Student combines morphology and scientific context into a coherent explanation.

Approximate SOLO connection:

- Relational

## Level 4: Transfer

Student uses known morphology to interpret or apply an unfamiliar term.

Approximate SOLO connection:

- Extended Abstract

Include this rubric only where useful.

# 15. Mystery Science Word

This should be one of the flagship activities.

Example:

## BIOLUMINESCENCE

Known clues:

BIO = life

LUMIN = light

Provide a short stimulus describing an organism producing light.

Questions:

1. What do you predict bioluminescence means?
2. Which word part gave you the strongest clue?
3. What evidence from the stimulus confirms or changes your prediction?
4. What might "luminescent mineral" mean?
5. Why does BIO matter in one term but not the other?

Use morphology as evidence, not as a memorization test.

# 17. Which Meaning Fits?

Create selected-response activities without pretending they are official SST questions.

Example:

A student reads:

"The plant's leaves contain microscopic openings."

MICRO means small.

Which meaning best fits MICROSCOPIC?

A. Related to very small things
B. Related to heat
C. Related to water
D. Related to movement

Then ask:

**What evidence helped you decide?**

Require reasoning after selection.

# 44. Content Quality Rules

Every word-part entry must pass these checks:

1. Is the morpheme meaning accurate enough for classroom use?
2. Is it useful in science?
3. Does it appear across multiple useful terms or in an important disciplinary term?
4. Is the grade-band placement reasonable?
5. Are example words real?
6. Does the science definition match the actual science concept?
7. Does the activity avoid implying that etymology equals scientific definition?
8. Is an etymological caution needed?

Do not add obscure roots merely to make the list larger.

Prioritize utility.

# 70. Example BOY → MOY → EOY Sequence

Use `GEOTHERMAL`.

## BOY

GEO means Earth.

THERM means heat.

What do you predict GEOTHERMAL relates to?

## MOY

Show a diagram of hot underground rock and water.

Ask:

How do the word parts GEO and THERM connect to what the diagram shows?

Use evidence.

## EOY

Present a new location where underground temperatures are unusually high.

Ask:

How might geothermal energy be useful at this location?

Explain how both the word and the scientific evidence support your answer.

This illustrates the desired progression.

# 71. Example Grade 6–8 Sequence

Use `ENDOTHERMIC` and `EXOTHERMIC`.

## BOY

Identify:

ENDO

EXO

THERM

## MOY

Compare temperature data before and after two reactions.

Connect the vocabulary to the data.

## EOY

Give an unfamiliar investigation.

Students infer whether a process is endothermic or exothermic and justify their reasoning.

Morphology assists the reasoning but does not replace the science.

---

# 72. Example Biology Sequence

Use `HOMEOSTASIS`.

Students identify:

HOME/O = similar/stable

STASIS = standing/state

Then provide a body-temperature regulation scenario.

Students must explain:

* Why homeostasis is an appropriate term
* What evidence shows regulation
* How the idea might apply to blood glucose or water balance

Do not define homeostasis solely through etymology.

# 75. Error Prevention

Watch for common morphology mistakes.

Examples:

* Treating spelling resemblance as genuine shared morphology
* Giving false Greek/Latin meanings
* Splitting words incorrectly
* Giving folk etymologies
* Assuming modern scientific definitions equal literal historical roots
* Using medically specialized roots without reason
* Introducing terms far above the science grade band

Every questionable entry should be researched before inclusion.

# 81. Final Product Principle

The resource should help a student move from:

> "I don't know that science word."

to:

> "I recognize part of it. I can make a prediction. Now I can use the science evidence to see whether my prediction makes sense."

That is the central instructional purpose of Break It, Build It.

Morphology opens the door.

Science evidence confirms the meaning.

ACE moves students from identifying the pieces to connecting them and eventually applying what they know to something unfamiliar.

The SST connection should reinforce that progression without reducing science instruction to test preparation.
