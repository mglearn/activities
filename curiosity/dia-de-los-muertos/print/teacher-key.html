Día de los Muertos — Teacher Key

Printable answer key and teaching notes. Treat Día de los Muertos as a living tradition of remembrance — not "Mexican Halloween," a costume theme, or a single uniform practice — and keep regional and family variation visible.

2. Map the Tradition — Teacher background (no single answer)

This is a notice-and-wonder task with no single right answer; assess observations and comparisons. The map is illustrative, not exhaustive.

  • Michoacán — Pátzcuaro & Janitzio: all-night candlelit cemetery vigils; Purépecha traditions.
  • Oaxaca: marigold-filled altars and lively street processions (comparsas).
  • Mexico City & nearby Mixquic: large cemetery observances.
  • U.S. diaspora (Los Angeles, San Antonio, Chicago and others): public celebrations hosted by museums and neighborhoods.

Inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2008; rooted in Indigenous Mexican communities and spread through migration. Do not generalize "all Mexicans do X" — practices vary by region and family, and not everyone participates.

Key call-outs: La Catrina is a modern satirical image (Posada's early-1900s "Calavera Garbancera," later named by Diego Rivera), not an ancient goddess. "Mexican Halloween" is a misconception — a distinct tradition of remembrance with pre-Hispanic and Catholic roots. "Depends" items honor genuine variation; celebrate it rather than seeking one "right" answer.

4. Pattern & Design Hunt — Design principles (no single answer)

Notice-and-wonder; assess observation and inference. Design principles to look for:

  • Repetition: rows of small repeated diamonds and half-circles create a steady rhythm.
  • Symmetry: a vertical line of symmetry — the left and right halves mirror each other.
  • Focal point: a central medallion of curling, flower-like shapes draws the eye first.
  • Color: bright paper contrasts with the wall, setting a lively mood.
  • Symbol & structure: a wide framing border and scalloped bottom edge; cut-out holes let light pass through as the banner moves.

Art extension: when students design their own patterns, have them invent respectful motifs — do not copy sacred or personal imagery from a real family's altar. Keep the focus on principles, not on reproducing specific images.

5. Memory Story — Privacy reminder & rubric

Privacy rule: students are never required to share a personal loss. Fictional or historical subjects are always welcome. Model with a historical or fictional example first so no one feels pressured toward personal grief.

Creation task — no single answer key. Suggested rubric:

  • Names a clear subject to honor (real, historical, or fictional — student's choice).
  • Completes ONE format: a six-word remembrance, a 3–5 sentence mini story, OR an illustrated memory card with caption.
  • Explains why the subject is worth remembering.
  • Names one symbol they would add and gives a reason.
  • Connects back to remembrance, not spectacle, as the core of the tradition.
